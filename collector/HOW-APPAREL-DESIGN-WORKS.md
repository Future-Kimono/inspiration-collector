# How apparel design actually works — and where our collector fits

Written for Ladana, August 2026. The point of this document is to give you the industry vocabulary and the standard process, so that when you choose how to build the Inspiration Collector you are choosing against a real model rather than guessing.

---

## Part 1 — The gold standard process

Every established clothing brand — Nike, Lululemon, Gymshark, LSKD, and at smaller scale Shoyoroll and Gold BJJ — runs some version of the same seven stages. The names differ, the stages don't.

### Stage 1 · Trend research and market intelligence

Two separate inputs, deliberately kept separate:

- **Trend research** looks outward at what the world is doing — colour forecasts, competitor drops, adjacent categories, street style, graphic design.
- **Consumer intelligence** looks at what your own buyers say — reviews, complaints, returns data, forum threads, support tickets.

Big brands buy this. A WGSN subscription (the industry-standard trend forecasting service) runs roughly £10–15k a year and tells you what colours and moods are coming 12–18 months out. Nobody at Future Kimonos' scale buys that. **This stage is exactly what our collector automates**, and it's the stage most small brands skip entirely — which is why so much BJJ gear looks like other BJJ gear.

Output of this stage: a moodboard and a written point of view.

### Stage 2 · The concept or seasonal brief

A short document that fixes the direction before anyone draws anything. It answers: who is this for, what's the story, what's the palette, how many pieces, what's the price point, when does it drop.

This is the stage where "buckets" get turned into a decision. You don't design from 400 saved images — you design from a brief that says *this season is washed greyscale, heavy type, no illustration.*

This is the stage Future Kimonos is missing most. Without it, inspiration collecting never converges on a product.

### Stage 3 · Design development

Sketches, then flats, then colourways. Two terms worth knowing:

- **Flats** (or technical drawings) — a flat, front-and-back line drawing of the garment with no body in it. This is the shape.
- **Colourways** — the same design in different colour combinations. One design, five colourways, is five SKUs for one design cost. This is the single biggest efficiency lever in apparel, and it's why brands obsess over palettes.

For a sublimated rash guard, the design *is* the artwork file wrapped onto the pattern pieces. There's no fabric development, no trim sourcing, no fit engineering — which is why a graphics-led brand can move fast where a technical brand can't.

### Stage 4 · The tech pack

The specification handed to the factory: measurements at every size, fabric composition, stitch types, print placement, artwork files at production resolution, labelling. If the tech pack is wrong, the sample is wrong.

### Stage 5 · Sampling

Factory makes a physical sample. You fit it, wear it, wash it, comment, and they revise. Typically two to three rounds. This is where most timeline slips, and it's the stage AI cannot compress — cloth has to physically exist.

### Stage 6 · Range review and buy

The team sits with all the samples together and cuts. A good range review kills 30–50% of what was developed. This is deliberate: designing more than you launch is how you get a strong range rather than a complete one.

Your "James and I pick the best 2" step *is* a range review. That's the right instinct.

### Stage 7 · Launch

Product photography, copy, site build, email, paid, organic. Then the sell-through data comes back and becomes an input to Stage 1 of the next cycle. That loop closing is what separates brands that get sharper from brands that just keep releasing.

## Part 2 — The vocabulary that will make you sound like you've done this

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Moodboard** | Curated images fixing the *feeling* of a range before any design exists |
| **Colourway** | One design rendered in a different colour combination |
| **Flat / technical drawing** | Line drawing of the garment shape, no model |
| **Tech pack** | The factory specification document |
| **Sublimation** | Dye is vaporised into polyester fibre — print can't crack or peel, but only works on synthetics and only on white base cloth |
| **Placement print** | Artwork sits in one defined area (chest, back panel) |
| **All-over print (AOP)** | Artwork covers the entire garment across seams — harder, more expensive, more premium-looking |
| **Panel** | An individual pattern piece; sublimated designs are authored per panel |
| **Drop** | A dated release of a defined set of products |
| **Carryover** | A product that stays in the range across seasons |
| **Range architecture** | The deliberate mix of core, seasonal and limited product in a collection |
| **Sell-through** | Percentage of units sold within a period — the number that judges a design |

## Part 3 — Where an AI collector genuinely helps, and where it doesn't

**Genuinely helps**

- Stage 1, almost entirely. Breadth of scanning, consistency of doing it daily, and never getting bored.
- Extracting *ingredients* rather than whole designs — exact hex values, pattern logic, layout structure.
- Consumer intelligence at volume: reading hundreds of reviews and forum comments and reporting what actually repeats.
- Stage 2 drafting: turning a month of collected findings into a proposed seasonal brief for you and James to argue with.

**Helps partially**

- Stage 3 mockups. AI can produce a convincing visual of a rash guard concept. It cannot produce a production-ready artwork file aligned to real pattern pieces — that still needs a designer working to the factory's template.

**Does not help**

- Stages 4 through 6. Tech packs, sampling and fit are physical and specification-critical.

**The honest framing for James:** this doesn't replace a designer. It removes the research bottleneck and the blank-page problem, so that when design time is spent, it's spent on a direction that already has evidence behind it.

## Part 4 — What "good" looks like for our version

Mapped onto your proposed workflow:

```
Collector researches daily          → Stage 1, automated
You star ~10 of 50                  → Stage 1 curation, human taste
Collector learns from your stars    → the system gets sharper over time
Monthly: propose a seasonal brief   → Stage 2, drafted by AI, decided by humans
Convert to 5-10 mockups             → Stage 3 concepting
You and James pick 2                → Stage 6 range review, moved earlier
Those become proper designs         → hand to a designer with the brief attached
```

The one thing to add to your original sketch is **Stage 2**. Going straight from starred images to mockups skips the step where you decide what the range is *about*. That step is cheap, takes an hour a month, and is the difference between a collection and a pile of products.

## Part 5 — A note on storage, since you raised it

You're right that most of what gets collected should be thrown away. That's normal and healthy — a good moodboard is the survivors of a much larger cull.

The way to get that without a storage problem is to **reference by default, save on approval**:

- Every day's finds are displayed by pointing at the image where it already lives on the competitor's own server. Nothing is downloaded. Zero storage cost.
- Only when you star something does it get saved permanently into a bucket folder.
- Unstarred items age out after a set window and the board stops showing them.

The trade-off is that a referenced image can disappear if the competitor pulls the product page. That's precisely why starring saves a permanent copy — the things you cared about are protected, the things you didn't aren't cluttering a drive.
